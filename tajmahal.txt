Taj Mahal Considered the peak of Mughal architecture, the Taj Mahal was built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in the 17th century to immortalize his consort Mumtaz Mahal.
Taj Mahal, white marble mausoleum complex in Agra, western Uttar Pradesh state, northern India. The Taj Mahal was built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan (reigned 1628–58) to immortalize his empress consort Mumtaz Mahal (“Beloved Ornament of the Palace”), who died in childbirth in 1631, having been the emperor’s inseparable companion since their marriage in 1612. One of India’s most iconic mausoleums, it is situated in the eastern part of the city on the southern (right) bank of the Yamuna (Jumna) River. Agra Fort (Red Fort), also on the right bank of the Yamuna, is about 1 mile (1.6 km) west of the Taj Mahal.
The Taj Mahal is one of the new Seven Wonders of the World
In its harmonious proportions and its fluid incorporation of decorative elements, the Taj Mahal is distinguished as the finest example of Mughal architecture, a blend of Indian, Persian, and Islamic styles. Other attractions include twin mosque buildings (placed symmetrically on either side of the mausoleum), gardens, and a museum. One of the most beautiful structural compositions in the world, the Taj Mahal is also one of the world’s most iconic monuments, visited by millions of tourists each year. The complex was designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1983.

The inspiration for the Taj Mahal was a verse by Bibadal Khan, the imperial goldsmith and poet. The vision behind the construction was to make an earthly replica of Mumtaz Mahal’s celestial abode in paradise. The plans for the complex have been attributed to various architects of the period, though the chief architect was probably Ustad Aḥmad Lahawrī, an Indian of Persian descent. The five principal elements of the complex—main gateway, garden, mosque, jawāb (literally “answer”; a building mirroring the mosque), and mausoleum (including its four minarets)—were conceived and designed as a unified entity according to the tenets of Mughal building practice, which allowed no subsequent addition or alteration. Building commenced about 1632. More than 20,000 workers were employed from India, Persia, the Ottoman Empire, and Europe to complete the mausoleum itself by about 1638–39; the adjunct buildings were finished by 1643, and decoration work continued until at least 1647. In total, construction of the 42-acre (17-hectare) complex spanned 22 years.
Resting in the middle of a wide plinth 23 feet (7 meters) high, the mausoleum proper is of white marble that reflects hues according to the intensity of sunlight or moonlight. It has four nearly identical facades, each with a wide central arch rising to 108 feet (33 meters) at its apex and chamfered (slanted) corners incorporating smaller arches. The majestic central dome, which reaches a height of 240 feet (73 meters) at the tip of its finial, is surrounded by four lesser domes. The acoustics inside the main dome cause the single note of a flute to reverberate five times. The interior of the mausoleum is organized around an octagonal marble chamber ornamented with low-relief carvings and semiprecious stones (pietra dura). Therein are the cenotaphs of Mumtaz Mahal and Shah Jahan. Those false tombs are enclosed by a finely wrought filigree marble screen. Beneath the tombs, at garden level, lie the true sarcophagi. Standing gracefully apart from the central building, at each of the four corners of the square plinth, are elegant minarets.
Flanking the mausoleum near the northwestern and northeastern edges of the garden, respectively, are two symmetrically identical buildings—the mosque, which faces east, and its jawāb, which faces west and provides aesthetic balance. Built of red Sikri sandstone with marble-necked domes and architraves, they contrast in both color and texture with the mausoleum’s white marble.
The garden is set out along classical Mughal lines—a square quartered by long watercourses (pools)—with walking paths, fountains, and ornamental trees. Enclosed by the walls and structures of the complex, it provides a striking approach to the mausoleum, which can be seen reflected in the garden’s central pools.
The southern end of the complex is graced by a wide red sandstone gateway with a recessed central arch two stories high. White marble paneling around the arch is inlaid with black Qurʾānic lettering and floral designs. The main arch is flanked by two pairs of smaller arches. Crowning the northern and southern facades of the gateway are matching rows of white chattris (chhattris; cupola-like structures), 11 to each facade, accompanied by thin ornamental minarets that rise to some 98 feet (30 meters). At the four corners of the structure are octagonal towers capped with larger chattris.

Two notable decorative features are repeated throughout the complex: pietra dura and Arabic calligraphy. As embodied in the Mughal craft, pietra dura (Italian: “hard stone”) incorporates the inlay of semiprecious stones of various colors, including lapis lazuli, jade, crystal, turquoise, and amethyst, in highly formalized and intertwining geometric and floral designs. The colors serve to moderate the dazzling expanse of the white Makrana marble. Under the direction of Amanat Khan al-Shirazi, verses from the Qurʾān were inscribed across numerous sections of the Taj Mahal in calligraphy, central to Islamic artistic tradition. One of the inscriptions in the sandstone gateway is known as Daybreak (89:28–30) and invites the faithful to enter paradise. Calligraphy also encircles the soaring arched entrances to the mausoleum proper. To ensure a uniform appearance from the vantage point of the terrace, the lettering increases in size according to its relative height and distance from the viewer.
Current issues:
Over the centuries the Taj Mahal has been subject to neglect and decay. A major restoration was carried out at the beginning of the 20th century under the direction of Lord Curzon, then the British viceroy of India. More recently, air pollution caused by emissions from foundries and other nearby factories and exhaust from motor vehicles has damaged the mausoleum, notably its marble facade. A number of measures have been taken to reduce the threat to the monument, among them the closing of some foundries and the installation of pollution-control equipment at others, the creation of a parkland buffer zone around the complex, and the banning of nearby vehicular traffic. A restoration and research program for the Taj Mahal was initiated in 1998. Progress in improving environmental conditions around the monument has been slow, however.
From time to time the Taj Mahal has been subject to India’s political dynamics. Night viewing was banned there between 1984 and 2004 because it was feared that the monument would be a target of Sikh militants. In addition, it increasingly has come to be seen as an Indian cultural symbol. Some Hindu nationalist groups have attempted to diminish the importance of the Muslim influence in accounting for the origins and design of the Taj Mahal.

Mughal architecture, building style that flourished in northern and central India from the mid-16th to the late 17th century under the patronage of the Mughal emperors, a line of Muslim rulers of Turkic-Mongol origin. The Mughal period marked a new phase of Islamic architecture in northern India, in which both religious and secular buildings shared an architectural vocabulary derived from Persian traditions, especially those developed under the Turkic conqueror Timur in Samarkand (ancestral home of the first Mughal emperor, Babur) and in Safavid Iran (where the second Mughal emperor, Humayun, lived in exile).
Mughal architecture developed a number of distinctive features over time, including prominent central arches in mosques, a feature derived from Samarkand’s Timurid architecture; iwans (tall vaulted halls recessed into a wall and open on one side) inspired by Safavid Iranian architecture; bulbous double domes; and formal, symmetrical planning, as seen in the hasht bihisht (“eight paradises”) layout, in which a central domed chamber is surrounded by eight subsidiary rooms. Mughal buildings were also often integrated with a geometrically ordered char bagh (“four-part garden”) layout, in which water channels divide the garden into quadrants. It is also characterized by the use of decoration involving the combination of red sandstone and white marble, pietra dura inlay, and carved jalis (latticed screens carved into stonework).

In 1526 Babur founded the Mughal dynasty in India with his victory over Sultan Ibrahim Lodi of Delhi in the Battle of Panipat. He continued to engage in battles to expand his territory in India until his death in 1530, but he noted an interest in constructing gardens and mosques in his autobiography, the Baburnama. Babur commissioned early Mughal structures, such as the mosques at Panipat (completed 1528–29) and Sambhal (1530), now in Haryana and Uttar Pradesh states, respectively. These mosques used architectural features such as masonry gates and circular domes, especially those with octagonal bases, favored by earlier dynasties of the Delhi Sultanate, a series of Indo-Islamic dynasties ruling northern India from the 13th to the 16th century. In addition to these, the Babri Masjid (1528–29) at Ayodhya in Uttar Pradesh, attributed to Babur’s general Mir Baqi, borrowed from the architectural tradition used by the Sharqi dynasty (1394–1479), a Muslim dynasty founded in Jaunpur by Malik Sarwar in the latter years of the Delhi Sultanate. The central bay of the mosque’s western prayer hall is marked by a tall entrance structure. This entrance has a rectangular opening topped by a projecting eave supported on heavy stone brackets. Its sides are decorated with projecting stone balconies and rows of recessed panels with archlike shapes, some of which contain carved floral medallions.

Babur, who traced his lineage to the Mongol conquerors Genghis Khan and Timur, also introduced features borrowed from the Timurid style used in Central Asia, especially Samarkand. The qibla walls (niched walls oriented toward Mecca for prayer) in his mosques featured a grand central hallway ending in a raised arch, a departure from the regular recesses used in mosques constructed earlier in the region. The mosque at Panipat also featured a plasterwork relief design on the small side domes, designed with netlike patterns connecting the arches that supported the dome, a transitional feature that replaced the hard-angled squinches of preceding eras.
Babur also pioneered the traditional Mughal garden layout, with its high-walled enclosures, geometric planning, and char bagh design. The Bagh-i Nilofar, in Rajasthan, is an example of such a garden attributed to Babur. The Bagh-i Babur (“Garden of Babur”) he commissioned close to Kabul is one of the most well preserved, illustrating the formal quadripartite layout that became central to later Mughal garden design. These features drew on the Timurid garden traditions of Samarkand. Babur faced challenges in recreating the well-irrigated gardens of Central Asia in the drier conditions of northern India and settled for building his gardens close to rivers, including Aram Bagh (now Ram Bagh), built in 1526 on the banks of the Yamuna River at Agra, Uttar Pradesh, and diverting water for the channels using networks of terraces and underground canals called qanats.
Humayun, Babur’s successor, spent much of his first reign (1530–40) fighting political rivals and Afghan forces. In 1539 he was forced into exile for a prolonged period after losing the empire to Sher Shah of Sur in the Battle of Chausa, limiting opportunities for sustained architectural patronage. Before his exile, however, he established his capital on the banks of the Yamuna River in modern-day Delhi. Called Din Panah, the city featured fortified walls and three gateways richly decorated with red sandstone and marble. Sher Shah partly dismantled the city some time after the Battle of Chausa and established his own capital, Shergarh, in its place. The remains of Din Panah and Shergarh survive as Purana Qila (“Old Fort”).
Another structure that has survived from Humayun’s rule is a mosque in Kachpura village near Agra. The central bay of the mosque’s qibla wall takes the form of a raised central arch, a feature continued from Babur’s reign. There are also a number of unmarked tombs in Delhi that date to Humayun’s reign, known by local names such as Sabz Burj (“Green Tower”) and Nila Gumbaz (“Blue Dome”). They adhere to a Timurid architectural plan, especially in their use of iwans.
Humayun regained the throne in 1555 after a prolonged exile, but his restoration was short-lived. The following year, in 1556, he died after a fall down the staircase of his library in Purana Qila, bringing an abrupt end to his reign.

The reign of Humayun’s son Akbar saw the successful expansion and consolidation of the Mughal Empire, along with administrative and revenue reforms that strengthened state finances. These changes brought the wealth and stability that enabled monument building on a grand scale. Among the most notable monuments of this period was Humayun’s Tomb, commissioned not by Akbar but by his stepmother, Bega Begum, in 1569. The structure uses a plan called hasht bihisht (“eight paradises”), derived from the architecture of Isfahan and Shiraz, both now in Iran. The tomb stands in the center of a char bagh layout and rests on a large stone plinth that serves as a functional base, housing the emperor’s burial chamber while elevating the tomb above its surroundings and enhancing its visual impact. The superstructure of the tomb thus rests above the actual burial chamber. This use of a plinth became a distinctive feature of later Mughal architecture. It is topped by a double dome, in which the inner dome is hemispherical. This allows the outer dome to be shaped independently and raised higher since it can withstand more load, contributing to the bulbous, onionlike shape that became characteristic of many Mughal monuments.
Muqarnas
Muqarnas are a three-dimensional geometrical ornamentation used in the transitional zones of arches, vaults, and domes, found in monuments as far apart as Syria and India. They are structural projections in which a tier projects farther than the one below it, creating a cascading visual effect. Humayun’s Tomb is a key Mughal monument that uses this feature.
Akbar expanded and reinforced existing forts erected during the Delhi Sultanate (13th to 16th century) era or earlier, including those at Agra and Lahore. Agra Fort was commissioned in 1565 and built on the site of a preexisting fort called Badalgarh and is a massive red sandstone fortress-palace enclosed by a 1.5-mile (2.5-kilometer) wall. The main entrance, known as the Delhi Gate, is decorated with stone carvings, relief work, and white marble inlay against red sandstone. Both stone and marble decorations feature geometric designs punctuated by floral motifs. Panels above doorways feature animals and birds, some of them fantastical, such as the gaja-vyala (a winged, elephant-headed creature with the body of a lion or tiger) fighting hordes of elephants. The Lahore Fort, rebuilt by Akbar beginning in 1566, developed into a major imperial complex in red sandstone, strategically located close to the Grand Trunk Road (the principal historic road linking northern Kabul in Afghanistan with Chittagong in Bangladesh) and between the Mughal strongholds of Kashmir, Kabul, and Multan.

One of Akbar’s most expansive architectural projects was the building of a new capital city, Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra, which had been Babur’s capital. The city’s early Mughal buildings use arches only sparingly, relying instead on post-and-lintel construction. They are built of red sandstone or white marble and feature a range of structural and decorative experiments. These include bracketed supports, which project outward from a wall or post to support the eaves of the arcades surrounding the courtyard of the grand mosque in the city, the Jama Masjid (1571; “Congregational Mosque”), and red sandstone carvings of Persian and Arabic calligraphy atop arches throughout the mosque complex.
The palace complex within the city reflected the more settled nature of Akbar’s court following his consolidation of Mughal authority across much of northern India and the development of a stable administrative system. Spaces in the royal palace became more organized, governed by fixed rules. This is seen in structures such as the Diwan-i Khas (“Hall of Private Audience”), a hall reserved for nobles and members of the royal family. The compact plan of this hall is organized around a central pillar with an elaborately carved capital supporting radiating stone walkways. Large residential units, such as the structure commonly identified as Jodha Bai’s palace (traditionally associated with a Rajput consort of Akbar, though the identification is uncertain), are arranged around spacious courtyards. Jodha Bai’s palace includes an enclosed zenana (private space in a household reserved, in this case, for women of the royal household and their attendants) organized around inward-looking courtyards and screened with finely carved jalis to ensure privacy. This concern for seclusion is further reflected in garden spaces such as the zenana bagh, or “Mariam’s Garden,” a fully enclosed garden reserved for the royal women. Laid out on a modified char bagh plan across two terraces, it incorporated controlled entryways, water channels, and a covered bathing tank connected to the residential quarters by a private passage.
The Jama Masjid, with its monumental Buland Darwaza, a victory gate commemorating Akbar’s successful military campaign in Gujarat in 1573, is one of the finest mosques of the Mughal period. Within the mosque, a marble tomb surrounded by a walkway with finely carved marble jalis is dedicated to the memory of the Sufi saint Salim Chishti, whom Akbar revered.
Bhool Bhulaiya
Adham Khan, Akbar’s foster brother and a powerful noble early in Akbar’s reign, became a contentious figure at court after murdering Akbar’s trusted minister Ataga Khan in 1562. Akbar ordered Khan’s execution and afterward commissioned a tomb for him near Delhi. Called Bhool Bhulaiya (a Hindustani term for a maze), the tomb is notable for the main building’s octagonal base and large circular dome. These features were associated with dynasties such as the Surs and Lodis, regarded by the Mughals as rivals, and have been seen as a deliberate reference to Adham Khan’s betrayal.
Akbar left the city when he shifted his capital to Lahore about 1584–85 to oversee campaigns to secure his empire’s northwestern frontier against threats from Uzbek forces (a Central Asian power controlling Transoxania) and instability in Kabul following the death of his half brother Mirza Hakim, the Mughal governor of Kabul who had earlier challenged Akbar’s authority. Fatehpur Sikri was completely abandoned by 1610, supposedly because of an inadequate water supply, though experts suggest that the royal family may have simply lost interest in it.
Architecture under Jahangir
In 1605 Akbar began to build a mausoleum for himself at Sikandra, a site about four miles (seven kilometers) northwest of Agra, which his son Jahangir completed by 1613. The complex is laid out as a large garden tomb, entered through a monumental gateway and set within a char bagh plan. At its center stands a five-story structure built primarily of red sandstone with white marble inlay. Each successive story recedes in size, creating a terraced effect, with arcaded galleries and a prominent central arch on the lower levels. The topmost story, added by Jahangir, is constructed entirely of white marble and takes the form of an open pavilion rather than an enclosed chamber; at the corners rise four slender marble minarets. Unlike later Mughal tombs, the structure lacks a dominant central dome and does not follow the conventional hasht bihisht plan. Instead, its flat oblong roof incorporates curved forms that anticipate the so-called Bengal roof—a sloping form derived from the curved hut roofs of Bengal, which were designed to shed water from heavy monsoon rains. Its inclusion in Mughal architecture followed the Battle of Rajmahal (1576), in which the Mughal Empire defeated the Bengal Sultanate and annexed the region.

During Jahangir’s reign (1605–27), Mughal architecture became more elaborate in ornamentation and material, notably through the increased use of white marble and pietra dura, a technique of inlaying semiprecious stones into stone surfaces. This technique, known locally as parchinkari, was subsequently developed into a distinctive feature of later Mughal decoration, as seen in the tomb of Jahangir’s father-in-law Itimad ud-Daulah at Agra, commissioned in 1622 by Nur Jahan, his daughter and one of Jahangir’s wives.

Shalimar BaghJahangir built Shalimar Bagh, inspired by Persian gardens, for his wife, Nur Jahan, in 1619.
The Shalimar Bagh, a royal garden in Srinagar, Kashmir, was laid out by emperor Jahangir in 1619 and expanded in 1630 under Shah Jahan. It also served as an imperial summer residence for the Mughal court. Adapted from the char bagh layout to suit Kashmir’s mountainous terrain, the garden is organized along a central axial water channel, called Shah Nahar (“Royal Stream”), descending through three terraced levels. These terraces were differentiated into an outer garden featuring a public hall, an inner garden centered around a private hall, and zenana spaces along the terrace. The design integrates flowing water, cascades, fountains, and tree-lined avenues with distinctive elements such as chini khanas (arched niches behind waterfalls) and marble pavilions.

Mughal architecture reached its maturity during the reign of the emperor Shah Jahan (1628–58). The Taj Mahal at Agra, commissioned by Shah Jahan in memory of his consort Mumtaz Mahal and built between about 1632 and 1647, represents the culmination of Mughal architectural achievement. Gates to the east, south, and west (the Fatehbadi, Sirhi, and Fatehpuri gates, respectively) lead into the forecourt, from which a central gateway opens onto a large char bagh garden. The white marble mausoleum at the far end of this garden is distinguished by its perfect symmetry, a central double dome, and four minarets. Its surfaces feature pietra dura inlay with semiprecious stones and Quranic calligraphy. The mausoleum rests on a large stone plinth of the type first used for Humayun’s Tomb, elevating the structure and enhancing its prominence. It houses the graves of Mumtaz Mahal and Shah Jahan, while the marble mausoleum above contains cenotaphs to serve as their symbolic memorials. To the west of the mausoleum is a mosque adjacent to the complex wall. It faces east across the mausoleum to a guest house that functions as a jawab (“answer;” a building facing the mosque), which completes the symmetry of the structural layout.

Delhi's Red FortOriginally called Qila-i Mubarak, the Lal Qila, or Red Fort, at Delhi was built by Shah Jahan (1638–48) as the citadel of his new capital, Shahjahanabad.
Shahjahanabad, Shah Jahan’s new capital at Delhi, was completed in 1648. Its citadel, the Qila-i Mubarak (“Blessed Fort”), now the Lal Qila or Red Fort, was planned along the Yamuna River. Notable spaces include the Diwan-i Am (“Hall for Commoners,” the hall of public audience) with its raised throne platform and the richly ornamented Diwan-i Khas (“Hall for Nobles,” the hall of private audience). The imperial apartments and guest palaces, notably the Diwan-i Khas, were distinguished by their opulent decoration, including pietra dura inlay. A flowing water channel called the Nahr-i Bihisht (“Stream of Paradise”) provided both aesthetic enhancement and natural cooling to the private chambers.
The urban plan of Shahjahanabad also incorporated major commercial and garden spaces patronized by members of the imperial family. Jahanara Begum, Shah Jahan’s eldest daughter, laid out the grand Chandni Chowk (“Moonlit Square”), a boulevard that became the city’s principal market axis, while Roshanara Begum, one of Shah Jahan’s younger daughters, commissioned a large garden, now Roshanara Bagh, in the northern part of the city.

Jama MasjidOriginally called the Masjid-i Jahannuma (“World-Reflecting Mosque”), Delhi's Jama Masjid was commissioned by Shah Jahan near his capital, Shahjahanabad.
Near the Red Fort, the Jama Masjid (originally the Masjid-i Jahannuma; “World-Reflecting Mosque”) was set on a high plinth and approached by broad steps. The mosque features a vast courtyard accommodating about 25,000 worshippers and a monumental eastern gateway once reserved for royalty. Built in red sandstone with marble accents, it includes tall minarets, a large prayer hall, Persian inscriptions, and three marble domes.

Shah Jahan’s successor, Aurangzeb, reigned from 1658 to 1707. He was more orthodox than his predecessors and devoted much of his reign to prolonged military campaigns in the Deccan. These campaigns placed significant strain on imperial resources and administration, contributing to a reduction in large-scale architectural patronage. Although he commissioned fewer monumental works, his religious commitments are reflected in the construction of mosques. Notable examples include the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore, one of the largest mosques in the Mughal Empire, distinguished by its vast courtyard and red sandstone construction with marble inlay, and the Moti Masjid (“Pearl Mosque”) built within Delhi’s Red Fort as a smaller, elegantly proportioned structure in white marble. It is named for the small white domes atop the structure, markedly more restrained than the monumental features of Aurangzeb’s predecessors.

Bibi Ka MaqbaraBuilt during the reign of Aurangzeb, Bibi ka Maqbara is a Taj Mahal–like mausoleum built in honor of his wife, Dilras Banu Begum.
The Bibi ka Maqbara (“Tomb of the Lady”) was commissioned during Aurangzeb’s reign at Aurangabad (now Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, Maharashtra) in 1679 in memory of his wife, Dilras Banu Begum. The mausoleum echoes the design of the Taj Mahal, with its central dome, four minarets, and broad square plinth, but it is more modest in scale. This reduction reflects the financial pressures associated with prolonged Deccan campaigns.
In the 18th century, the political authority of Mughal rulers after Aurangzeb weakened significantly. Delhi was invaded by the Persian ruler Nadir Shah in 1739. The city was sacked, and immense wealth—including the imperial treasury—was carried off, severely undermining the empire’s resources and prestige. At the same time, European trading companies, especially the British East India Company, were expanding their political and military influence, gradually taking control of key regions and further eroding Mughal power. As central authority declined, imperial architectural patronage diminished.

Development of the Mughal Empire
Mughal dynasty, line of Muslim rulers of Turkic-Mongol origin who ruled most of northern India from the early 16th to the mid-18th century. After that time it continued to exist as a considerably reduced and increasingly powerless entity until the mid-19th century. The Mughal dynasty was notable for its more than two centuries of effective rule over much of India; for the ability of its rulers, who through seven generations maintained a record of unusual talent; and for its administrative organization. A further distinction was the attempt of the Mughals, who were Muslims, to integrate Hindus and Muslims into a united Indian state.
Mughal Empire
At its height the Mughal Empire encompassed most of the Indian subcontinent. By the late 16th century, the Mughal ruler Akbar had extended Mughal dominion across northern India from the Bay of Bengal to the Arabian Sea. Subsequent rulers pushed the frontiers southward. In the late 17th century Aurangzeb conquered the Deccan sultanates, extending Mughal rule farthest into the south and bringing the empire to its greatest overall territorial extent. In this century Mughal India accounted for roughly 24 percent of the world’s gross domestic product (GDP), making it one of the richest economies globally. Historians estimate the empire’s population to have been between 100 million and 150 million in the late 17th century.
Gunpowder Empires
The expression gunpowder empires was popularized by American historian Marshall G.S. Hodgson in the late 20th century and refers to a group of dominant states in the Islamic world and neighboring regions during the late medieval and early modern periods, characterized by their effective use of gunpowder weapons in the expansion of territory. Empires such as the Ottomans in Anatolia, the Safavids in Persia, and the Mughals in India are considered gunpowder empires.
The Mughal Empire, like its contemporary gunpowder empires, made effective use of firearms and centralized military power in the 16th and the 17th centuries. The Mughal Empire’s centralized administration and large, well-equipped army allowed it to integrate diverse regions into a single political framework. Babur, the first Mughal ruler, imported Timurid statecraft derived from the traditions of his Central Asian ancestors but adapted it to Indian conditions. Babur and his son Humayun established only a fragile hold, yet the next generation under Akbar firmly consolidated Mughal rule through institutional and inclusive governance. The Mughal emperors cultivated ties of loyalty with local nobles whose political standing relied not on landownership but on their proximity to the king. Their influence, in turn, was largely determined by their military value and usefulness to the ruler. The Mughals employed Persian as the official court language. The Mughal state endured (at least in name) until 1857, when the last emperor, Bahadur Shah II, was deposed by the British colonizers of India. During its peak the empire was a dominant force in South Asia—politically, economically, and culturally.
Mughal rulers
The BaburnamaMeeting between Babur and the sultan Ali Mirza near Samarkand (now in Uzbekistan), illustration from the Baburnama (“History of Babur”), c. 1590; in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York City.
The Mughal dynasty was founded by a Chagatai Turkic prince named Babur (reigned 1526–30), who was descended from the Turkic conqueror Timur (Tamerlane) on his father’s side and from Chagatai, the second son of the Mongol ruler Genghis Khan, on his mother’s side. Babur’s father, Umar Shaykh Mirza, ruled the small principality of Fergana to the north of the Hindu Kush mountain range; Babur inherited the principality at a young age, in 1494.
In 1504 he conquered Kabul and Ghaznī and established himself there. In 1511 he captured Samarkand, only to realize that, with the formidable Safavid dynasty in Iran and the Uzbeks in Central Asia, he should rather turn to the southeast toward India to have an empire of his own. As a Timurid, Babur had an eye on the Punjab region, part of which had been Timur’s possession. He made several excursions in the tribal habitats there. Between 1519 and 1524—when he invaded Bhera, Sialkot, and Lahore—he showed his definite intention to conquer Hindustan, where the political scene favored his adventure.
Having secured the Punjab, Babur advanced toward Delhi, garnering support from many Delhi nobles. He routed two advance troop contingents of Ibrahim Lodi, Delhi’s sultan, and met the sultan’s main army at the First Battle of Panipat. By April 1526 he was in control of Delhi and Agra and held the keys to conquer Hindustan.
The Rajput confederacy, however, under Rana Sanga of Mewar threatened to revive their power in northern India. Babur led an expedition against the rana and crushed the rana’s forces at Khanua, near Fatehpur Sikri (March 1527), once again by means of the skillful positioning of troops. Babur then continued his campaigns to subjugate the Rajputs of Chanderi. When Afghan risings turned him to the east, he had to fight, among others, the joint forces of the Afghans and the sultan of Bengal in 1529 at Ghaghara, near Varanasi. Babur won the battles, but the expedition there too, like the one on the southern borders, was left unfinished. Developments in Central Asia and Babur’s failing health forced him to withdraw. He died in Agra in December 1530.
 
Humayun
Babur’s son Humayun inherited the hope rather than the fact of empire, because the Afghans and Rajputs were merely restrained but not reconciled to Mughal supremacy by the Mughal victories at Panipat (1526), Khanua (1527), and the Ghaghara (1529). Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, encouraged by Afghan and Mughal émigrés, challenged the Mughals in Rajasthan, and, although Humayun occupied Gujarat in 1535, the danger there ended only with Bahadur’s death in 1537. Meanwhile, an Afghan soldier of fortune, Sher Shah of Sur, had consolidated his power in Bihar and Bengal. He defeated Humayun at Chausa in 1539 and at Kannauj in 1540, expelling him from India.

Reaching Iran in 1544, Humayun was granted military aid by Shah Tahmasp and went on to conquer Kandahār (1545) and to seize Kabul three times from his own disloyal brother, Kamran, the final time being in 1550. Taking advantage of civil wars among the descendants of Sher Shah, Humayun captured Lahore in February 1555, and, after defeating Sikandar Sur, the rebel Afghan governor of the Punjab, at Sirhind, he recovered Delhi and Agra that July. Humayun was fatally injured by falling down the staircase of his library. His tomb in Delhi, built several years after his death, is the first of the great Mughal architectural masterpieces; it was designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1993.
